However, it turns out that λ is a relation number if and only if −λ is a root of a
generalized Chebyshev polynomial (see Theorem A below). A polynomial characterization
of the relation number has been considered previously by many authors [20, 24, 4, 2, 26], and
Chebyshev formulation appeared recently for the representation of the 2-bridge knot group
case [17]. It is interesting to notice that the coefficients of Bamberg polynomial has ‘cyclic
symmetry’ while those of Chebyshev has ‘linear symmetry’. In this paper we further show
that all the components w(X, Y )ij can be written in the Chebyshev polynomials explicitly
(see Theorem 3.2), which should be useful for a ‘membership problem’ [7, 25, 5]. The
Chebyshev setting brought us lots of advantages in the analysis for the proofs of Theorem
B and C below.
   In 1955, Brenner [5] demonstrated that Gλ is free for |λ| ≥ 4 following [25], then in 1958,
Chang-Jennings-Ree [6] extended this by proving that Gλ is free for λ outside three open
discs of radius 2 centered at (−2, 0), (0, 0), and (2, 0) respectively. In 1969, Lyndon and
Ullman [20] further contributed by establishing that Gλ is free if either of the following
two conditions hold:
      • λ is not within the interior of the convex hull of the set consisting of the circle
         |z| = 2 together with the two points z = ±4.
      • |λ ± i| ≥ 1 and |λ ± 2| ≥ 2.
After that, Ignatov proved that Gλ is free if any one of the following conditions holds (see
[14] and [15]):
      • |λ − 2| > 1 and 2 ≤ |Re(λ)| < 52 .
      • |λ| > 2 and |Im(λ)| > 1.
   Now it is well known that λ is free (and discrete) when it lies in the closure of the Riley
slice in C [18] and the set of relation numbers is dense in the exterior of the Riley slice ‘in
a strong sense’ [21]. (See also [1] for a recent summary for the discrete case.)
   There have also been numerous results about the rational relation numbers. It is known
earlier that the set of relation numbers are densely distributed inside the unit circle [23]
and also dense on the interval (−4, 4) [6]. Then one might ask about the non-freeness of
rational numbers in that interval, and Lyndon and Ullman actually verified that a family
of rational numbers and some others are non-free. Since then the list grows gradually by
the works of many authors, and there are currently no known rational numbers within the
interval (−4, 4) being identified as free. (see Gilman’s paper [11] for a summary up to that
time.) Hence it has been conjectured that all rational numbers within the interval (−4, 4)

relation number belongs to Rk for some positive integer k, and the set of relation numbers

problem, Theorem C, follows in the next section. The initial idea for the proof is the ob-
servation that, as some of ni ’s approach the infinity, the corresponding roots of Chebyshev
polynomials approach the roots of some lower degree Chebyshev polynomials.
   In Section 6, we prove Theorem C by establishing an exhaustive    √ algorithm to find a

nonzero integer sequence n of a given length k satisfying sk+1 ( −λ) = 0 for a given
λ ∈ C assuming k is the minimal such length. The idea for the proof is to consider
a (generalized) continued fraction associated to λ and n. We show that the shortest
continued fraction whose value is ∞ gives the sequence we want to find. The algorithm
is designed to systematically compute the maximum absolute value of continued fractions
of a given length i, denoted as Mi , and it checks whether Mi equals to ∞ or not. The
values of Mi are calculated in sequential order, which establishes that Mi is achieved by
an integer sequence within a bounded region of Zi determined using M1 , M2 , · · · , Mi−1 .
These also essentially determine Ni in Theorem B.
