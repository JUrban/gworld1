                                                                             Abstract
                                                  We give an O(n log3 (n))–time algorithm for the word problem in the
                                                mapping class group of a compact surface.

                                          keywords. mapping class group, word problem, train-tracks, curve shortening,
                                          half-GCD

                                          1     Introduction
                                          Suppose that S is a compact surface, possibly with boundary. We define
                                          MCG(S), the mapping class group of S, to be the group of isotopy classes of
                                          homeomorphisms of S. Since this group is finitely generated [10] one may ask
                                          for a solution to its word problem: an algorithm that, given a word of length n
                                          in the generators and their inverses, decides whether the resulting mapping class
                                          is trivial.
                                              Our main result is the following.
                                          Theorem 1.1. There is an algorithm that solves the word problem for MCG(S)
                                          in O(n log3 (n)) time. Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed
                                          polynomial in |χ(S)|.
                                              This answers a question of Farb [15, Question 3.1]; it also gives the best
                                          solution to date to Thurston’s Problem 20 [45, Page 380]. We prove Theorem 1.1
                                          using divide-and-conquer, ideas of Dynnikov [13], and the following.
                                          Theorem 1.2. There is an algorithm to compute the geometric intersection num-
                                          ber of two curves (given in ∆–coordinates) in O(n log2 (n)) time. Furthermore,
                                          the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in |χ(S)|.
                                              Our proof of Theorem 1.2 is inspired by the half-GCD algorithm [29]; this
                                          uses divide-and-conquer to accelerate the euclidean algorithm. As a special case
                                          of Theorem 1.2, we also obtain the best solution to date for a question (posed as
                                          a “remarque”) of [17, Exposé 4, page 66].
                                          Theorem 1.3. There is an algorithm for curve shortening in S which runs in
                                          O(n log2 (n)) time. Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed
                                          polynomial in |χ(S)|.
                                              This work is in the public domain.

1.4    Reduction to a special case
The general case of Theorem 1.1 reduces, in linear time, to the following special
case:
   • The surface S is connected and oriented,
   • the surface S has χ(S) ≤ −2,
   • the mapping class f preserves orientation, and
   • the mapping class f preserves the boundary of S, componentwise.
We henceforth assume that S = Sg,b : the surface of genus g with b boundary
components. We use the notation Mod(S) := MCG+      ∂ (S) for the group of such
mapping classes. It is a theorem from Dehn’s Breslau lectures [11] (see also
[10, 27, 26]) that Mod(S) is finitely generated by Dehn twists. Fix F , a finite
generating set of Mod(S).

1.5    Corollaries
Remark 1.6. There is another common variant of the mapping class group,
MCG+ (S, ∂S), where homeomorphisms and isotopies fix ∂S pointwise. The
word problem in this case is reduced to the previous one by attaching pairs of
pants to all boundary components of S.
Remark 1.7. Thus for any fixed value of r we obtain an O(n log3 (n)) algorithm
to solve the word problem in Br : the braid group on r strands. This answers
Problem B13 on the list maintained by Shpilrain [39].
Remark 1.8. The order of torsion in MCG(S) is bounded, solely in terms of the
topology of S; this follows from the classification of hyperbolic orbifolds and a
result of Nielsen [32, page 24, Staz]. Thus we have an O(n log3 (n)) algorithm to
decide whether a given mapping class has finite order.

1.9    History and other work
In a pair of papers [9, 10], and in his Breslau lectures [11], Dehn
   • sets out the word problem for finitely generated groups,
   • defines the mapping class group MCG(S),
   • proves that the mapping class group is finitely generated1 , and
   • gives two solutions to its word problem.
  1 Dehn assumes that S is oriented. For non-orientable S, finite generation is due to

Chillingworth [7].


                                          2
Dehn’s first solution relies on the action of the mapping class group on π1 (S).
Since Dehn’s work predates the invention of computational complexity by many
decades [18], he gives no time estimates. However, a naı̈ve analysis shows that
his first algorithm is exponential time. It is accelerated to polynomial time
by [38, Theorem A.8].
    Dehn’s second solution relies on what he calls the arithmetic field C(S): (es-
sentially) the set of isotopy classes of simple closed multi-curves in S. Dehn shows
that a choice of pants decomposition for S equips C(S) with intersection/twist
coordinates. Furthermore, the natural action of MCG(S) on C(S) is piecewise
linear in these coordinates. A naı̈ve analysis shows that his second algorithm is
quadratic time.
    Parts of Dehn’s work were recovered, and parts greatly extended, by Lickorish
(1960s) and Thurston (1970s). For a discussion, see Stillwell’s translator’s notes
in [11]. Other quadratic-time algorithms for the word problem, since Dehn’s,
include [30, 40, 41, 22, 42]. Most recently Dynnikov [13] has given a quadratic-
time algorithm using curve shortening. His paper is one of the inspirations for
our work; we refer to the appropriate sections of [13] as they arise. We refer to
Erickson–Nayyeri [14] for a history of curve shortening, an extensive bibliography,
and state-of-the-art algorithms in the RAM model.
    Programs that solve the word problem (often as just a small part of their
functionality) include [35, Appendix C, Twist], [28, BH], [6, XTrain], [20, Trains],
[21, Dynn], [3, Flipper], and [4, Curver]. Other relevant programs include [46,
Branched] and [2, Teruaki].

2.1    Matrices and further reduction
Suppose that d is a non-negative integer. We treat GL(d, Z) as both a motivating
example and as a useful tool. Fast integer multiplication gives fast matrix
multiplication, as follows.
Corollary 2.2. Suppose that A and B are matrices in GL(d, Z) with ||A||, ||B|| ≤
n. Then we can compute the product A · B in O(n log(n)) time. Furthermore,
the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in d.
    From [25], and [33, Proposition 2], we have the following.
Proposition 2.3. Suppose that (En , . . . , E2 , E1 ) is a list of elementary ma-
trices in GL(d, Z). Then we can compute their product in O(n log2 (n)) time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in d.
Proof. We split the list (Ei ) in half and recurse. The two subproducts have
complexity at most O(n), by induction. Thus we can compute the final product
in time O(n log(n)) by Corollary 2.2.
    Let t(n) bound the time needed to compute the product of n elementary
matrices. We deduce that

                          t(n) ≤ 2t(n/2) + O(n log(n))

Thus t(n) = O(n log2 (n)) by the master theorem [8, Theorem 4.1].
Corollary 2.4. The word problem for GL(d, Z), as generated by elementary
matrices, can be solved in O(n log2 (n)) time. Furthermore, the implied constants
are bounded by a fixed polynomial in d.
    Recall that F is our fixed finite generating set for Mod(S).
Corollary 2.5. Suppose that fn , . . . , f2 , f1 ∈ F ∪ F −1 is a list of generators
and inverses of generators. Then the action of f := fn ◦ · · · ◦ f2 ◦ f1 on H1 (S)
(given as an integer matrix) can be computed in O(n log2 (n)) time.
    Using this, from now on we may assume that certain mapping classes act
trivially on H1 (S).


3     Curves, pants decompositions, and coordinates
Let C(S) denote the set of isotopy classes of essential non-peripheral simple
closed multi-curves in S. As usual and when needed, we will blur the distinction
between a multi-curve and its isotopy class. For α and β in C(S) we use ι(α, β)
to denote their geometric intersection number : the minimal intersection number
among representatives of α and β. We use C0 (S) ⊂ C(S) for the subset of curves
(multi-curves with one component).

3.1    Cuffs, dual curves, and double duals
In our construction of pants decompositions, dual curves, and double duals we
closely follow [17, Exposé 4, Section III].
     Fix a pants decomposition P ⊂ C0 (S) of S: a maximal collection of disjoint
non-parallel curves. Note that |P | = 3g − 3 + b where g is the genus of S and b
is its number of boundary components. The curves α ∈ P are the cuffs of the
pants decomposition. Each component of S − P is a pair of pants: its completion
(in the induced path metric) is a copy of S0,3 . Note that |S − P | = 2g − 2 + b.
     For each cuff α ∈ P define Xα to be the non-pants component of S − (P − α).
Thus Xα is either a once-holed torus or a four-holed sphere. Following [17,
page 62], we arrange matters so that all of the Xα are four-holed spheres.
     A curve β ∈ C0 (S) is dual to α relative to P if β lies in Xα and satisfies
ι(α, β) = 2. We fix Q ⊂ C0 (S) to be a collection of dual curves relative to P ,
one per cuff.
     Lastly, for each cuff α ∈ P and its dual β ∈ Q, their double dual is γ := Tα (β);
the image of β under the right Dehn twist about α. We fix R ⊂ C0 (S) to be the
set of double dual curves.

4.1    Index
Suppose that Q is a region: a compact riemannian surface with piecewise smooth
boundary (perhaps empty). We assume that at each non-smooth point of ∂Q
the two adjacent arcs of the boundary make an interior angle of either zero or
90 degrees. We call the former points (outward) cusps and the latter (outward)
corners. We define the index of Q following [31, page 57]:
                  1                         1
index(Q) := χ(Q) − (number of cusps in ∂Q) − (number of corners in ∂Q)
                  2                         4
The second and third terms give a combinatorial version of the contribution of
geodesic curvature in the Gauss–Bonnet formula. Note that if Q = R ⊔ R′ , then
index(Q) = index(R) + index(R′ ).
    With Q a region as above, suppose now that α is a properly embedded simple
arc, or closed curve, in Q. If α is an arc, then we suppose that its endpoints
   • avoid the cusps and corners of ∂R and
   • are tangent or perpendicular to ∂R.

In a small abuse of notation, we define Q − α to be the completion (in the
induced path metric) of Q − α. We can now state the “additivity” property of
the index [31, pages 57–58].
Lemma 4.2. With Q and α as above, index( Q − α ) = index(Q).

4.3    Train tracks
A train track τ in a surface S is essentially an embedded graph with smoothings
at its vertices; see [44, Section 8.9] or [34, 31] for further background. Here is a
definition suited to our needs.

Definition 4.4. Suppose that τ ⊂ S is a closed subset. Suppose that S(τ ) is a
finite subset of τ ; these are the switches of τ . The points of τ − S(τ ) are the
branch points of τ . We say that τ is a train track if it satisfies the following.
   1. Every branch point x of τ has a disc neighbourhood Ux (in S) so that
      τ ∩ Ux is a smooth simple arc properly embedded in Ux .

   2. Every switch s ∈ S(τ ) has a disc neighbourhood Us (in S) so that τs =
      τ ∩ Us is a smooth graph properly embedded in Us . The graph τs has one
      vertex of valence three (at s), three vertices of valence one (on ∂Us ), and
      three edges, called branch-ends. We require that the three components
      of Us − τs have indices one-half, one-half, and zero, respectively. See
      Figure 4.5. The component of Us − τs with index zero is called the cusp
      at s. The branch-end not contained in the boundary of the cusp at s is
      called large; the two branch-ends contained in the boundary of the cusp
      are called small.
   3. Suppose that C is a component of S − τ . Suppose that R is the region
      obtained by taking the completion of C (in its induced path metric. Then
      R has negative index.



                                         8
                              Figure 4.5: A switch


The components of τ − S(τ ) are called the branches of τ ; we gather these into
a set B(τ ). A branch is large if both of its branch-ends are large; a branch is
small if both of its branch-ends are small; a branch is mixed if it has one large
and one small branch-end.
    Note that a train track may have connected components without switches.
These components are necessarily simple closed curves, no two of which are
isotopic to each other (or to a component of ∂S). Additionally, train tracks in S
have at most B := 6(3g − 3 + b) branches.

4.6    Weightings
Definition 4.7. Suppose that τ is a train track. A weighting (on τ ) is a function
µ : B(τ ) → Z. We denote the space of weightings on τ by W (τ ). We refer to
the pair (τ, µ) as a weighted train track.
   A weighting µ on τ gives a multi-curve C(µ) exactly when:
   • (non-negativity) µ(b) ≥ 0 for each b ∈ B(τ ) and
   • (switch equality) for any switch s ∈ S(τ ), if a, b, and c are the branch-ends
     at s, with a large and with b and c small, then we have µ(a) = µ(b) + µ(c).
We build C(µ) by taking µ(b) arcs parallel to b and gluing ends according to the
switch equality. We use V (τ ) ⊂ W (τ ) to denote the cone of such weightings.

5     Improving
In this section we describe moves that can be applied to a tight pair of weighted
train tracks τ = (σ, µ, τ, ν). These moves are analogous to those used by Agol–
Hass–Thurston [1, Section 4] for simplifying weighted train tracks. See also the
work of Erickson–Nayyeri [14].
    We will show how the correct sequence of moves can be used to reach a


                                        14
crossing pair of weighted train tracks. In particular, this process can be applied
starting from a substandard pair of weighted train tracks since these are tight
by Proposition 4.13.
    When (σ, µ) and (τ, ν) correspond to multi-curves, these moves preserve the
geometric intersection number of C(µ) and C(ν). Thus we can then immediately
calculate ι(C(µ), C(ν)) via ⟨·, ·⟩ once we have reached a crossing pair.
    From now on we will assume that each train track τ comes with a cho-
sen ordering of its branches (b1 , b2 , . . . , bk ). This allows us to explicitly write
weightings on τ as vectors and various linear transformations between them as
matrices. We use these to measure the complexity of weightings and such linear
transformations. Since train tracks have at most B branches, all of the following
matrices are at most B × B.

7     Remarks and questions
Remark 7.1. To implement Algorithms 3 and 4 efficiently one should take into
account that:
    • The constants that appear throughout the paper were chosen to shorten
      the proofs; several of them can be reduced.
    • In Algorithm 3 if ⌈⌈T3 ⌉⌉∩ ≤ ⌈⌈T1 ⌉⌉∩ − ω(T1 )/E then we may immediately
      return U2 ◦ U1 and so skip Lines 8 – 12.
    • In Algorithm 4 it is likely more efficient to revert to Algorithm 2 once
      ||τ 1 ||∩ is small (below some uniform bound) instead of recursing all the
      way to the base case where ||τ 1 ||∩ = 0.
    • Branches of T = (σ, u, τ, v) which are disjoint from σ ∩ τ are never modified
      within Algorithm 3. Therefore if these weights are passed by reference then
      this algorithm can be improved to run in O(m log2 (m)+log(n) log(m)) and
      so Algorithm 4 can be made to run in O((m + log(n)) log2 (m)) time.
Remark 7.2. If we allows stops – vertices of valence one on ∂S – in our train tracks,
then we can represent integral lamination with arcs. In this case, Algorithm 4
generalises to compute ι(α, β) for any pair of integral laminations, including when
α and / or β are arcs, in O(n log2 (n)) time where n = ||∆(α)|| + ||∆(β)||.
Remark 7.3. Suppose that G is a group and that F is a finite generating set for
G. An exponent word [19, Definition 3.1] over F has the following form.

                               w = fkpk · · · f2p2 · f1p1

where fi ∈ F and pi ∈ Z are integers. (These also Pcalled zipped words in [13,
page 183].) The complexity of such a w is ||w|| := i ||pi ||.
   We claim that for d ≥ 3 the word problem for exponent words in GL(d, Z)
generated by elementary matrices can also be solved in O(n log2 (n)) time where
n = ||w||. We prove this by reducing to Proposition 2.3. We first express each


                                          29
syllable Eipi as a product of O(log(pi )) elementary matrices [36, Lemma 2.3].
The product is obtained by converting the given binary representation of pi
into its Zeckendorf representation: a sum of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers.
Preprocessing each pi this way takes O(||pi || log2 (||pi ||)) time [5, Section 1.7.2].
Thus preprocessing the entire exponent word takes O(n log2 (n)) time, proving
the claim.
    In similar fashion, we obtain a O(n · poly(log(n))) time solution to the word
problem for exponent words in the mapping class group when the generators
are Dehn twists by applying Remark 3.12 to the base cases (Algorithm 1) of
Algorithm 1.
    We end by noting that in both cases the computational complexity of the word
problem for exponent words is very sensitive to the exact choice of generators.
   In work in progress, the first author and Webb, have given a polynomial-time
algorithm for the conjugacy problem in the mapping class group.

Question 7.4. Is there an algorithm for the conjugacy problem in the mapping
class group whose running time is subquadratic in n?
   Dynnikov gave a uniform quadratic-time solution (in the RAM model) to
the word problem in braid groups [12, page 215, Proposition 1.13]. That is, the
constants do not depend on the number of strands. Dylan Thurston [42, page 2]
points out that this technique generalises to all surfaces. This leads us to ask
the following.
Question 7.5. Is there an algorithm for the word problem in the mapping class
group whose running time is subquadratic in n and has constants independent
of S?

walking ∂R anti-clockwise recording the colours of the boundary segments that
we encounter (r, b or g for red, blue or gray) and the types of transitions between
them (-, L or V for smooth, corner or cusp). We call an occurrence of r-g-b or
b-g-r a colour change in ∂R. Under this scheme the four cases of Figure 4.14
