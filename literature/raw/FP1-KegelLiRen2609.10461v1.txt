                                                                                        1. Introduction

                                          Many undecidability results in topology are proved by showing that a hypothetical algorithm for a
                                          topological decision problem would yield an algorithm for a known undecidable problem in group
                                          theory. This approach, pioneered by Markov [Mar58], has led to numerous undecidable problems
                                          in topology. These group-theoretic decision problems are also of intrinsic interest in combinatorial
                                          group theory. A natural quantitative question is how small a finite presentation can be while still
                                          exhibiting algorithmically undecidable behavior.


                                          1.1. Algebra. The word problem for finitely presented groups, originating in the work of Dehn
                                          [Deh11], is one of the classical decision problems in group theory. A fundamental negative result was
                                          obtained by Novikov [Nov52; Nov55] and independently by Boone [Boo59], who constructed finitely
                                          presented groups with unsolvable word problem. Subsequent work sought increasingly economical
                                          examples: Boone [Boo59] obtained an example with 32 defining relators, Collins reduced this to 18
                                          [Col66] and subsequently to 14 [Col69], and Borisov [Bor69] obtained an example with 12 relators.
                                          The latter was the smallest number of relators previously known for a finitely presented group with
                                          unsolvable word problem. Our first result improves upon this.
                                          Theorem 1.1. There exists a 3-generator 9-relator group with unsolvable word problem.

                                          The triviality problem for finitely presented groups is to determine, from a finite presentation,
                                          whether the presented group is trivial. Following Novikov’s proof of the undecidability of the
                                          word problem [Nov55], Adian proved that the triviality problem is undecidable, as are many other
                                          recognition problems for finitely presented groups [Ady55; Ady57]. Rabin independently obtained
                                          a general version of this result [Rab58], now known as the Adian–Rabin theorem.
                                          The undecidability of the triviality problem can be quantified using the notion of Adian–Rabin
                                          families.
                                          Definition 1.2. A recursively enumerable family of finite group presentations P is called an Adian–
                                          Rabin family if there is no algorithm that takes as input P ∈ P and decides whether or not the
                                          group presented by P is trivial.

By the work of Adian [Ady55; Ady57] and Rabin [Rab58], the set of all finite group presentations
forms an Adian–Rabin family. To measure the “smallness” of Adian–Rabin families, one may ask
for such families with additional restrictions, for example, with a fixed (small) number of relators, or
a fixed deficiency1. A longstanding open problem asks whether there exists an Adian–Rabin family
consisting entirely of balanced presentations. The best previously known constructions are due to
Tancer [Tan23], who constructed an Adian–Rabin family of deficiency 9, and Gordon [Gor22], who
constructed one with 13 relators. Our next results improve upon both bounds.
Theorem 1.3. There exists an Adian–Rabin family of 4-generator, 11-relator group presentations.
Theorem 1.4. There exists an Adian–Rabin family of 2-generator, 10-relator group presentations.

1.2. Topology. We say that a closed PL n-manifold M is PL-unrecognizable if there is no algo-
rithm that, given a PL triangulation of a closed manifold X, decides whether or not X is PL-
homeomorphic to M . Similarly, M is topologically unrecognizable if there is no algorithm deciding
whether or not X is homeomorphic to M . In dimensions n ≤ 6, every PL n-manifold admits a com-
patible smooth structure, unique up to diffeomorphism [Mil11]. Consequently, PL-unrecognizability
and smooth unrecognizability are equivalent in these dimensions.
By Perelman’s proof of the Geometrization Conjecture [Per02; Per03b; Per03a], every closed man-
ifold of dimension at most three is recognizable [Kup19]. In contrast, the fundamental work of
Markov [Mar58] and Novikov (see [VKF74; CL06]) shows that there are unrecognizable manifolds
in every dimension at least four. In fact, in dimensions greater than four, every nonempty closed
PL manifold is PL-unrecognizable [CL06].
In dimension four, the situation is more mysterious. It is a major open question whether S 4 is rec-
ognizable; see, for example, Problem 4.53 in the K3 problem list [BKR26]. Markov’s construction
implies that the existence of an Adian–Rabin family with r relators yields the smooth and topologi-
cal unrecognizability of #r (S 2 ×S 2 ) (see, for example, [FHK26]); the existence of such an r was first
shown in [Ady55]. Subsequent work reduced the number of S 2 × S 2 factors. In particular, Tancer
[Tan23] observed that a deficiency-d Adian–Rabin family yields the topological unrecognizability
of #d (S 2 × S 2 ) as a consequence of Freedman’s theorem [Fre82], and Gordon [Gor22] observed that
an r-relator Adian–Rabin family yields the smooth unrecognizability of #r−1 (S 2 × S 2 ) (although
he only stated this in the topological category). Using their constructions of Adian–Rabin fami-
lies mentioned above, they thereby showed the topological unrecognizability of #9 (S 2 × S 2 ) and
the smooth unrecognizability of #12 (S 2 × S 2 ), respectively. Our Theorems 1.3 and 1.4 yield the
following improvements.
Corollary 1.5. #n (S 2 × S 2 ) is topologically unrecognizable for n ≥ 7 and smoothly unrecognizable
for n ≥ 9.                                                                                        □
Remark 1.6. Using analogous arguments, one can also show that (#a CP2 )#(#b CP2 ) is topologically
unrecognizable for min(a, b) ≥ 7 and smoothly unrecognizable for min(a, b) ≥ 9.
Remark 1.7. Cameron Gordon informed us that he has independently obtained improvements on the
smallest known Adian–Rabin families in the spirit of Theorems 1.3 and 1.4, based on a modification
of Borisov’s group in [Bor69]. Using these improvements, he was able to show both the smooth
and the topological unrecognizability of #8 (S 2 × S 2 ). This will appear in an upcoming paper.

1.4. Proof outline of Theorem 1.1. We briefly discuss the proof of Theorem 1.1, which uses
a standard strategy in combinatorial group theory and builds directly on the ideas in Borisov’s
construction [Bor69]. We start with Borisov’s group Γ4 , which has unsolvable word problem [Bor69].
We then construct in Section 2 two HNN extensions
                                          Γ4 ,→ Γ5 ,→ Γ6 .
For the first extension, we identify two suitable subgroups of Γ4 and an isomorphism between them;
for the second, we do the same for two subgroups of Γ5 . The main technical step is to prove that
these subgroups have precisely the claimed presentations, with no additional relations. This is
carried out in Section 3. Once this is established, the embeddings follow from the standard theory
of HNN extensions, in particular, Britton’s lemma; see, for example, [Coh89, Theorem 32(ii) and
Proposition 34].
Since Γ4 embeds into Γ6 and has unsolvable word problem, Γ6 also has unsolvable word problem.
Finally, we simplify the presentation of Γ6 : seven relators are redundant, and six generator–relator
pairs can be eliminated by Tietze transformations. This leaves a presentation with three generators
and nine relators, proving Theorem 1.1.

1.6. Lean autoformalization. With the assistance of GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex, we formalized in
Lean 4 the proofs of Theorems 1.1, 1.3, and 1.4, together with all their dependencies, including
Matiyasevich’s 2-letter 3-relator semigroup/Thue system with unsolvable word problem [Mat95]
and Borisov’s construction [Bor69]. The complete formalization is available in the accompanying
GitHub repository [KLR26]. We briefly summarize the development process.


    (1) We first used GPT-5.6 Sol Pro to produce an informal proof of Theorem 1.1, which was
        recorded in a companion PDF.
    (2) We asked Codex to formalize the statements of the three main theorems. After some
        simplification, these occupy fewer than 80 lines of Lean code. We checked the resulting
        statements manually.
    (3) We then provided Codex with the informal proof and asked it to identify the main de-
        pendencies and construct the overall Lean proof skeleton. At this intermediate stage,
        five project-specific assumptions were represented by temporary Lean axioms. The most
        substantial ones concerned an undecidable 2-generator 3-relator semigroup/Thue system,
        initially attributed to Matiyasevich’s 1967 construction [Mat67], Borisov’s unsolvability re-
        duction from groups to semigroups/Thue systems, and the injectivity of the two further
        compressions from Borisov’s group Γ4 .
    (4) For each temporary axiom, we asked Codex to identify the relevant literature, collected
        the sources in a dedicated reference directory, and then asked it to formalize the required
        arguments. This discharged all but the Thue-system input. Codex traced the route through
        Matiyasevich’s 1967 construction back through earlier work to Novikov’s undecidability the-
        orem [Nov55], deciding that a direct formalization of that route would be disproportionately
        large.
    (5) To remove the remaining axiom, we followed a different route. Starting from a fixed Turing
        machine with an undecidable halting problem, Codex formalized Post’s machine-to-Thue
        construction [Pos47] and then Matiyasevich’s compression to a binary three-rule Thue sys-
        tem [Mat95]. This completed the proof of the required undecidability statement.
    (6) We prompted Codex to find the improvement of [Tan23, Theorem 9] that we used in the
        proof of Theorem 1.3 above. We asked Codex to formalize the reductions to Theorems 1.3
        and 1.4 following Tancer [Tan23] (with the improvement above) and Gordon [Gor22].
    (7) After the proof was complete, we asked Codex to audit the development for project-specific
        axioms and sorry declarations, remove redundant arguments, simplify proofs, and reorga-
        nize the repository. The complete proof contains approximately 28,000 lines of Lean code.
        A transitive axiom audit of the three main theorems reports only Lean’s standard principles
        propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound.
    (8) Finally, we verified that the formalization is accepted by the Lean Comparator [Böv25].

            Appendix A. A faithful encoding of Matiyasevich’s construction

In [Mat95, eq. (6)], a semigroup Σ of the form
                                         Σ = ⟨x, y | Fi ∼ Ei , i = 1, 2, 3⟩
was constructed such that

      (i) There exists a positive word P in x, y such that there is no algorithm to decide, given a
          word G, whether or not G ∼ P in Σ;
     (ii) F1 = xxyxy, E1 = yxx, F2 = xxyy and E2 = yxx.

It is clear that Σ satisfies the properties (a) and (c) listed at the beginning of Section 2. The
property (b), however, is not immediately clear from the literature, as F3 , E3 and P were not
explicitly stated. Therefore we apply a faithful encoding to Σ to avoid having to determine these
words explicitly.
Consider the homomorphism η from the free semigroup ⟨x, y⟩ to the free semigroup ⟨s1 , s2 ⟩ defined
by
                                    η(x) = s1 s22 s1 , η(y) = s1 s32 s1 .
Put Fi := η(Fi ), Ei := η(Ei ) and P := η(P), and define
                                       Π := ⟨s1 , s2 | Fi ∼ Ei , i = 1, 2, 3⟩.
It is straightforward to see that Π satisfies both properties (b) and (c). For property (a), it suffices
to show the following:
                  SMALL UNDECIDABLE GROUPS AND UNRECOGNIZABLE 4-MANIFOLDS                         11


Proposition A.1. For any two positive words U and V in x and y, U ∼ V in Σ if and only if
η(U ) ∼ η(V ) in Π.

Proof. The forward implication is immediate. For the converse, observe that η is injective and that
every word in its image has a unique factorization into copies of η(x) and η(y). It suffices to show
that no occurrence of Fi or Ei can begin in the interior of a factor η(x) or η(y), so that we can
translate replacements Fi ↔ Ei in Π back into replacements in Σ. Indeed, both Fi and Ei begin
with s1 s2 , which occurs only at the very beginning of each η(x) and η(y) for an element in the
image of η since it must be of the form
                                   s1 sj21 s21 sj22 s21 · · · sj2n s1 ,   jk ∈ {2, 3}.            □
