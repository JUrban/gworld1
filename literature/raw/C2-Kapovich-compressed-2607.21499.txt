                                                                                                 1. Introduction
                                              Straight-line programs, a data-compression tool from computer science, are useful for algorithmic problems
                                           in group theory whose inputs may grow exponentially under algebraic operations. The word problem for
                                           Aut(Fr ), where Fr = F (x1 , . . . , xr ) has finite rank r ≥ 2, is typical: a product φn of n generators may send
                                           a basis letter xi to a word of exponential length, even when that word freely reduces to xi . Naive successive
                                           substitution may therefore require exponential time and space.
                                              Schleimer [17] instead represented φn (xi ) by a polynomial-size compressed word over Σr = {x1 , . . . , xr }±1
                                           and performed free reduction directly in compressed form. This gave deterministic polynomial-time algo-
                                           rithms for the word problems in Aut(Fr ), Out(Fr ), and Fr ⋊φ Z with φ ∈ Aut(Fr ). Briefly, a straight-line
                                           program (SLP) A over a fixed terminal alphabet Σ is an acyclic grammar with nonterminals A1 , . . . , Am and
                                           productions Ai → Wi , where Wi uses only terminals and earlier nonterminals. Its root Am produces a word

   Schleimer’s work initiated an active study of compressed group-theoretic problems; see Lohrey’s survey [10]
and the account of Lohrey–Schleimer [11]. Polynomial-time algorithms are known for compressed word,
conjugacy, simultaneous-conjugacy, and centralizer problems in hyperbolic groups [3]; the same work proves
NP-completeness of compressed knapsack in every infinite hyperbolic group. Polynomial-time algorithms are
also known for the compressed word and conjugacy problems in groups hyperbolic relative to free abelian
subgroups [4, 5], and for several compressed problems in finitely generated nilpotent groups [12]. For free
groups, Linton’s polynomial-time fully compressed subgroup-membership algorithm [9], with a fixed number
of compressed generators, is a key ingredient in our NP verifier.
   Strong hardness results are also known. Bartholdi, Figelius, Lohrey, and Weiß proved that the compressed
word problem is PSPACE-complete for several natural groups, including Thompson’s groups, the Grigorchuk
and Gupta–Sidki groups, and wreath products G ≀ Z with G finite non-solvable or free of rank at least
two [1]. Wächter and Weiß constructed an automaton group whose compressed word problem is EXPSPACE-
complete [19].
   We study compressed primitivity in Fr = F (Xr ), where Xr = {x1 , . . . , xr } and Σr = Xr ⊔ Xr−1 . An
element is primitive if it belongs to a free basis. For g ̸= 1, this is equivalent to ⟨g⟩ being a rank-one free
factor, or to g = φ(x1 ) for some φ ∈ Aut(Fr ). For an SLP A over Σr , let wA ∈ Σ∗r be its output and gA ∈ Fr
the represented element. The compressed primitivity problem asks whether gA is primitive.
   To place the problem in standard complexity classes, Definition 2.2 gives a canonical injective encoding
encr over a fixed alphabet Ωr . Put β(A) = | encr (A)|. Proposition 2.3 shows that
                              ∥A∥ ≤ β(A),        β(A) = O(∥A∥ log(∥A∥ + 2)) .
Thus the two size measures are polynomially equivalent. Complexity classes are measured using β(A), while
grammar-growth estimates use ∥A∥. Identifying an SLP with its code, define
                CPrimr = {encr (A) : A is an SLP over Σr and gA is primitive in Fr } ⊆ Ω∗r .
Invalid encodings are rejected.
   As usual, NP denotes the set of languages decidable in nondeterministic polynomial time, and P denotes
the set of languages decidable in deterministic polynomial time.
   Our main result, restated as Theorem 6.4, is:
Theorem 1.1. For every fixed integer r ≥ 2, the language CPrimr belongs to NP. Equivalently, the com-
pressed primitivity problem in Fr is decidable in nondeterministic polynomial time.
  For F2 = F (a, b), Theorem 8.7 gives the stronger conclusion:
Theorem 1.2. The compressed primitivity problem for F2 = F (a, b) is decidable in deterministic polynomial
time. Equivalently, CPrim2 ∈ P.
   The proof of Theorem 1.1 combines three ingredients: Schleimer’s compressed cyclic reduction [17],
Puder’s immediate-quotient criterion [14, 15], and Linton’s subgroup-membership algorithm for a fixed num-
ber of compressed generators [9]. In particular, Theorem 5.3 sharpens Puder’s primitivity criterion: instead
of a sequence of immediate quotients, it specifies pairs of vertices in the original cycle Sw labelled by a
cyclically reduced word w.
   We also prove that, for fixed r, Whitehead minimality—equivalently, automorphic minimality—of a com-
pressed conjugacy class is decidable in deterministic polynomial time (Theorem 3.9). The algorithm computes
the weighted Whitehead graph directly from the grammar.
   After the preliminaries in Section 2, Sections 4–6 prove Theorem 1.1. Section 3 is independent of that
proof, and Section 8 treats rank two.

minimality test recognizes a minimal class, and layered substitution evaluates a supplied sequence, but
neither bounds the number of descent stages.

   The results of this paper naturally raise the following questions:
Open problem 1.4. Let r ≥ 3 be a fixed integer. Does CPrimr belong to P? As an a priori easier question,
does CPrimr belong to coNP?
   Theorem 1.1 gives CPrimr ∈ NP. Membership in coNP would strengthen this, but is weaker than mem-
bership in P.
   Roig, Ventura, and Weil [16] solve Whitehead minimization for uncompressed words in polynomial time,
even when r is part of the input. For fixed r, the compressed version asks, given an SLP A, for an SLP B pro-
ducing a freely and cyclically reduced word minimal in the automorphic orbit of wA . Decompression followed
by Whitehead minimization gives a deterministic exponential-time solution; its optimality is unknown:
Open problem 1.5. Let r ≥ 2 be a fixed integer. What can be said about the complexity of the Whitehead
minimization problem for compressed words in Fr ?
   To the author’s knowledge, Problem 1.5 remains open even for F2 . Our deterministic polynomial time
primitivity algorithm in Theorem 1.2 uses combinatorics specific to primitive elements in F2 and does not
extend to other Aut(F2 )-orbits.

output-size, and certificate-size bounds may use either size measure. Explicit bit-operation bounds refer to

  An SLP is in Chomsky normal form if every production has one of the forms
                               Ai −→ z     (z ∈ Σr ),      Ai −→ Aj Ak        (j, k < i),
with the additional exceptional rule Am → ε permitted when the output word is empty. First replace every
terminal occurring in a right-hand side of length greater than one by a shared terminal nonterminal. Since
Σr is fixed, this adds only Or (1) new productions and right-hand-side symbols, while leaving the lengths of
the original right-hand sides unchanged. Long right-hand sides can then be binarized by introducing binary
trees of dummy nonterminals. Empty references can be deleted recursively, and unit productions can be
eliminated by redirecting references in the acyclic dependency order, without copying terminal expansions.
If the root expansion is empty, replace the program by the one-rule program A1 → ε; otherwise, discard
all empty and root-unreachable nonterminals. Relabel the remaining variables in dependency order. Thus
the total number of nonterminals and right-hand-side symbol occurrences grows by at most a linear factor.
Consequently every SLP of size n can be converted in polynomial time to an equivalent SLP in Chomsky
normal form of size O(n). This is the normal form used by Schleimer [17]. An SLP with m nonterminals in
this form has size O(m) and encoded length O(m log(m + 2)) by Proposition 2.3.

                       3. Compressed substitutions and Whitehead minimality
   For a conjugacy class [g] in Fr , let
           ∥[g]∥Xr = min{|u| : u ∈ Σ∗r is freely reduced and represents an element conjugate to g}.
Equivalently, if u is any word representing g, then ∥[g]∥Xr = | cycred(u)|. The automorphism group acts on
conjugacy classes by φ([g]) = [φ(g)]. We say that [g] is automorphically minimal if
                                ∥φ([g])∥Xr ≥ ∥[g]∥Xr             for every φ ∈ Aut(Fr ).
For fixed r, we test automorphic minimality in deterministic polynomial time by computing the weighted
Whitehead graph of the cyclically reduced compressed word and applying Whitehead’s length-change for-
mula.

                                    6. Polynomial-time verification
  We now convert Theorem 5.3 into an NP verifier, with r ≥ 2 fixed.
  We use the following consequence of Linton’s main algorithm.
Proposition 6.1 (Linton). Fix k ≥ 1. There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm with the following
input and output. The input consists of SLPs W, W1 , . . . , Wk over Σr . The algorithm decides whether the
element represented by W belongs to
                                      H = ⟨val(W1 ), . . . , val(Wk )⟩ ≤ Fr .
More precisely, Theorem 5.8 of [9] gives a bound of the form nO(k) in its input-size notation. If B denotes the
total encoded input length in our canonical encoding, the same estimate is B O(k) after the standard change of
encoding. If M is the total structural size of the input SLPs, Proposition 2.3 then gives B = O(M log(M +2)),
and hence also a bound M O(k) after absorbing the logarithmic factor into the constant hidden in O(k).
Corollary 5.9 of [9] gives polynomial time for fixed k.
   In the graph language of Section 4, the subgroup generators may be placed on compressed loops at one base
state, together with inverse loops, producing an involutive compressed nondeterministic automaton. Linton’s
algorithm performs compressed analogues of subdivision and Stallings folding and converts this object to a
compressed deterministic automaton representing the same subgroup. Membership of the compressed query
word is then tested without expanding any transition label. The proposition accepts arbitrary SLP words
over the involutive alphabet; if desired, each input word may first be freely reduced by Proposition 2.5(a),
without affecting the polynomial bound.

Remark 7.1 (endpoint verification and the sequential interpretation). The verifier checks only the endpoint
equality K = FI and does not explicitly construct the intermediate core graphs. Nevertheless, if

intermediate graphs or to run Linton’s algorithm separately on each Ki : verifying the endpoint certifies the
entire chain.
Remark 7.2 (bounded rank does not bound graph size). The rank bound alone is not a sufficient complexity
argument. The initial graph Sw has rank one but N vertices, where N may be exponential in the input SLP
size. The prefix-difference formulation avoids ever listing these vertices or edges. A vertex is named by an
O(log N )-bit position, and its path from the basepoint is represented by a polynomial-size prefix SLP.
Remark 7.3 (dependence on the ambient rank). The proof establishes an NP upper bound for each fixed r.
Linton’s quantitative bound is M O(k) , where M is the total structural size of the membership instance and
the subgroup is given by k compressed generators. Here k = s ≤ r, so the verifier has a bound of the form
M O(r) , and the exponent is constant once r is fixed. The constant hidden in this exponent is inherited from
Linton’s analysis and is not made explicit here. If r is included as part of the input, the same argument does
not give a verifier whose running-time exponent is independent of r.

Remark 8.9 (literal shortening). On a positive primitive conjugacy class with exponent-sum pair (P, Q),
Proposition 8.3 shows that the automorphism a 7→ ab−m performs exactly the proposed simultaneous can-
cellation of the common power bm . The b-block exponents are m or m + 1, but their cyclic order is the
additional arithmetic information supplied by the Christoffel formula. After the shear, the residual expo-
nents are 0 or 1, and the roles of a and b may be interchanged. Thus the Euclidean process does shorten a
primitive word by a uniform multiplicative factor at each batched stage. The algorithm above does not need
to recover the exponentially long block decomposition: it performs the same shears directly on the input
SLP and makes a single cyclic-length test at the end.
