                                           1            Introduction

                                           There is no algorithm to decide whether an arbitrary finitely presented group
                                           has nontrivial profinite completion, or equivalently, a nontrivial finite image
                                           [BW]. Within the universe of residually finite groups, however, the opposite is
                                           true: given that the finitely presented group G is residually finite, we note that
                                           G has a nontrivial finite image if and only if some generator of G is not the
                                           identity; and this is decidable by the naive routine of simultaneously listing (a)
                                           finite images and (b) consequences of the relations. Thus we can decide whether
                                           or not the profinite completion G  b is the trivial group.
                                               The profinite isomorphism problem asks: given two finitely presented groups,
                                           do they have isomorphic profinite completions? This is of course more demand-
                                           ing than the ‘profinite triviality problem’, and is undecidable even within the
                                           class of residually finite groups [BW2].
                                               It has long been known that among finitely presented residually finite groups,
                                           the polycyclic groups are particularly tractable. Around 40 years ago two major
                                           results were established:

                                           Theorem 1 [S2] The isomorphism problem for virtually polycyclic groups is
                                           decidable.

                                           Theorem 2 [GPS] The (relative) profinite genus of a virtually polycyclic group
                                           is finite.

                                               The former means that there exists a uniform algorithm that decides, given
                                           two finite presentations of virtually polycyclic groups, whether the two groups
                                           are isomorphic. (The fact that the presented groups are virtually polycyclic has
                                           to be given: it is in principle undecidable, by the Adian-Rabin Theorem, [LS]
                                           Chap. 4, §4.)
                                               Theorem 2 asserts that the class of all virtually polycyclic groups having a
                                           given profinite completion consists of finitely many isomorphism classes. In fact
                                           we can remove the word “relative”: the class of all finitely generated residually
                                           finite groups having the same profinite completion as a given virtually polycyclic
                                           group (consists of virtually polycyclic groups and) contains only finitely many

result of [SW], §4.
   The point of this note is to fill an obvious gap by establishing

Theorem 3 The profinite isomorphism problem for virtually polycyclic groups
is decidable.

    That is, there exists a uniform algorithm that decides, given two finite pre-
sentations of virtually polycyclic groups, whether the two groups have isomor-
phic profinite completions.
    Two groups have isomorphic profinite completions if and only if they have
the same finite images (‘isomorphism’ of profinite completions is supposed to
be topological isomorphism; for virtually polycyclic groups this is the same as
abstract isomorphism, cf. [S1] Chap. 10, Prop. 4). Thus there is a trivial
procedure that will determine the negation of profinite isomorphism: if G  b≇H   b
                                            m          m
we eventually find m ∈ N such that G/G ≇ H/H . However to establish a
positive result is harder. This is the opposite of the situation with the original
isomorphism problem, where an isomorphism, if one exists, will eventually be
found, while non-isomorphism is much harder to establish.
    Even given Theorems 1 and 3, there is no known procedure to determine
the class number of a polycyclic group, that is the cardinality of its profinite
genus. This number is related, in a messy way, to the class number of a certain
algebraic group (cf. [S1] Chapters 9, 10 and [PR] Chapter 8, where the authors
write “... computation of class number is a difficult, even hopeless, problem
in its most general formulation” ). It is tempting to guess that at least one of
the three algorithmic problems – ‘isomorphism’, ‘profinite isomorphism’, ‘class
number’ – should be reducible to the conjunction of the other two; but I don’t
see it.
    A guiding principle in number theory is that ‘local’ problems are supposed to
be easier than ‘global’ problems. To prove Theorem 3, we use the methodology
of Theorems 1 and 2 to reduce the question to a Diophantine problem; the result
then follows by the following very general result of James Ax. Let us call a finite
family of polynomial equations in finitely many variables with coefficients in Z
a Diophantine system. The system is locally solvable if it is solvable over Zp for
every prime p.

Theorem 4 [A] The local solvability of a given Diophantine system is algorith-
mically decidable.

    Ax remarks on the contrast between this fact and the (now known) unsolv-
ability of Hilbert’s 10th problem. The possibility of proving Theorem 1 rested
on the reduction of that question not to any old Diophantine problem, but to
one of a very special kind: namely an orbit problem for an arithmetic group
(see [S1] Chapter 8, §D). From this point of view, Theorem 3 should be seen as
the ‘easy cousin’ of Theorem 1: apart from the group theory (and, to be fair,
some nontrivial number theory coming from the proof of Theorem 2), it only
needs Theorem 4.

    The special case of all three theorems 1 - 3 relating to T -groups – torsion-
free finitely generated nilpotent groups – is discussed in Section 1 of [GS3]; in
that context, the group theory question translates quickly to a corresponding
arithmetical question via the Lie algebra. The analogous reduction in the general
polycyclic case is harder; but much of the hard work has essentially been done,
in the course of establishing Theorems 1 and 2.
    Both of those theorems proceed by first embedding a virtually polycyclic
group into GLn (Z) in a particularly rigid way: the question of isomorphism
between abstract groups is thereby translated into one of conjugacy between
subgroups of a linear group. The same method could be adapted to prove
Theorem 3, but the flexibility provided by Theorem 4 allows us to specify an
isomorphism more directly.

presentation of G is given; an element of G is given if it is given as a word in

generators, each of which is given.
